# What Technology Wants — Kevin Kelly
**Format**: md | **Pages**: not preserved | **Sections**: 14 | **Depth**: study

*Interpretive status: named frameworks and examples are attributed to this book; operational checks and critical limits are editorial synthesis, not quotations.*

## Mental Model (read first)
Kelly places individual tools inside the technium: a connected system of artifacts, ideas, institutions, and further tool-making. He argues that it exhibits long-run tendencies while leaving meaningful choices about particular forms and uses. The useful tension is between expanding society's possibilities and deliberately selecting the smaller set of technologies that fits a person's or community's life.

## Frameworks & Structure
### My Question and Origins: chapters 1–4
- **Technium** — The term includes hardware, software, culture, law, intellectual creations, and the institutions that produce more technology. It names the system rather than a single artifact. To understand a tool, trace the dependencies and feedback that make its use possible; a device's visible surface can conceal the consequential arrangement.
- **Inventing ourselves** — Humans and technologies develop through each other. Tools change how people live, communicate, and pursue possibilities; those changes motivate further tools. The argument resists treating technology as a detachable addition to a fully unchanged human life, while not eliminating human choices about its forms.
- **Seventh kingdom** — Kelly extends the evolutionary story into the made world, highlighting persistence, combination, and expanding variety. This is his large-scale interpretive lens, not a conventional biological classification or proof that artifacts have organisms' moral standing. Use it to examine system behavior without literalizing the metaphor.
- **Exotropy** — Kelly uses the term for increasing order and organized possibility, while acknowledging difficulties defining and measuring it. It is not a usable empirical index of social benefit. A claim that a technology increases complexity or information therefore still needs a separate account of what becomes better for whom.

### Imperatives: chapters 5–9
- **Deep progress** — Small net gains accumulate despite substantial costs and failures. Kelly emphasizes expanding options as a central benefit. Distinguish that criterion from distribution: an additional option available somewhere is not necessarily an option available to the person bearing a new burden.
- **Ordained becoming and convergence** — Repeated independent solutions in evolution and invention suggest that constraints make some broad forms more likely. This supports searching for recurrent functional pressures. It does not prove that a particular company, product, political arrangement, or timetable is predetermined.
- **Listen to the technology** — Observe a technology's characteristic strengths, costs, and directions of improvement rather than force it into an unsuitable role. The imperative is interpretive: identify the work a tool is good at and the dependencies it creates. It is not an instruction to accept whatever a vendor proposes.
- **Choosing the inevitable** — Broad trajectories leave room to choose implementations, defaults, combinations, and timing. A communication capability might appear in centralized or distributed forms with very different consequences. Make those forks explicit instead of treating the existence of a technical possibility as a completed social decision.

### Choices: chapters 10–12
- **The Unabomber Was Right** — The provocatively titled chapter engages the critique of technological dependence while rejecting the destructive response. Kelly takes the cost to autonomy seriously but argues against abandoning the wider expansion of possibilities. The chapter title is not an endorsement of violence or a claim that the critique is sufficient.
- **Lessons of Amish Hackers** — Amish communities illustrate selective, experimental adoption based on effects on community life. Their choices are not reducible to accepting old tools and rejecting new ones. Identify the practice being protected and observe how a particular use changes it; the same hardware can have different social arrangements.
- **Seeking conviviality** — Select tools that help people pursue worthwhile possibilities while steering their harmful expressions. Personal nonadoption can coexist with support for a broad technological society. The alternative to indiscriminate adoption need not be universal rejection or an attempt to live outside all technical dependence.
- **Proactionary Principle** — Kelly adapts Max More's proposal into five proactions: Anticipation; Continual Assessment; Prioritization of Risks, Including Natural Ones; Rapid Correction of Harm; Not Prohibition but Redirection. This is a normative approach to governance. Its appeal depends on realistic detection, correction, and accountability, not only on willingness to experiment.
- **Anticipation** — Use scenarios, forecasts, experiments, and other ways of imagining both benefits and harms. Actual evidence should carry more weight than speculation. Ask what happens under widespread use, not only in the first demonstration; the purpose is preparation for decisions rather than prediction of every detail.
- **Continual Assessment** — Keep testing a technology after introduction, across contexts and populations. An initial approval is not the end of learning. Assessment must be capable of changing practice; measurement without a responsible actor or a response mechanism can produce records without correction.
- **Prioritization of Risks, Including Natural Ones** — Compare technological risks with existing and naturally occurring risks, including the cost of inaction. Do not demand an impossible zero-risk alternative. At the same time, failure to quantify a novel hazard does not make its probability or consequence zero.
- **Rapid Correction of Harm** — Expect problems and design timely remedies and compensation. The key application question is whether correction is actually possible at the proposed scale. Irreversible damage, long detection lags, and harms borne by nonparticipants weaken a simple learn-by-deployment argument.
- **Not Prohibition but Redirection** — Kelly prefers finding a more beneficial task or expression for a problematic technology. Explore changes in use, defaults, scale, and organization. His broad skepticism about prohibition is a position to evaluate, not a settled law that every temporary restriction or ban must fail.

### Directions: chapters 13–14
- **Efficiency and opportunity** — Two of the thirteen named trajectories concern achieving more with fewer means and expanding the space of possible action. They are related but not identical: a highly efficient system can remove alternatives. Compare the available choices and who can exercise them, not only input savings.
- **Emergence and complexity** — More interconnection can produce behavior not obvious from the components. This widens possibility while making prediction and control harder. Identify interfaces and feedback rather than treating additional complexity as a benefit by itself.
- **Diversity and specialization** — The technium differentiates into more forms and specialized capabilities. These can complement one another, but specialization also increases dependence on other participants. A resilient choice should consider what happens when a crucial complementary capability disappears.
- **Ubiquity and freedom** — A capability's spread can make more choices available to more people. Yet ubiquity can also make refusal costly. Distinguish access to a tool from freedom over its use, and examine whether other viable arrangements remain possible.
- **Mutualism and beauty** — Kelly includes cooperative fit and aesthetic richness among technology's directions. These broaden evaluation beyond throughput. The application is to examine how a tool contributes to relationships and experience, rather than assume every technically advanced artifact realizes those values.
- **Sentience and structure** — Increasing responsiveness and organization are further proposed trajectories. Technical signs of sensing or adaptive behavior should not be confused with established consciousness. The philosophical proposal requires distinctions that a count of sensors or computations cannot resolve.
- **Evolvability** — A system can improve its ability to generate and retain useful variation. Favor arrangements that preserve learning, modular experimentation, and future options when they fit the task. An optimized but rigid arrangement may perform well now while disabling future adaptation.
- **Playing the Infinite Game** — The aim is to keep expanding the possibilities for further participation and creation. This differs from winning a fixed contest at everyone else's expense. In a concrete decision, ask whether today's gain preserves the conditions for others and future users to continue making worthwhile choices.

## Worked Example
Kelly's Amish inquiry treats seemingly inconsistent adoption choices as a clue rather than a reason for dismissal. A community may permit a powered implement yet reject a particular form of transport or household connection because the issue is what its use does to community dependence and daily life. Trials and community judgment, rather than a simple old/new rule, determine acceptance. The transferable method is to name the practice being protected, test a specific use, and retain the ability to limit or reshape it. A workplace or individual can borrow that deliberateness without importing Amish authority structures or assuming that all members experience the community's choices identically.

## Decision Rules & Judgment
- If a tool looks self-contained, map its material, institutional, and skill dependencies.
- If a broad tendency is described as inevitable, identify the implementations and defaults still open to choice.
- If seeking simplicity, select tools by the practices they support rather than by a blanket age or novelty rule.
- If invoking expanding options, ask whose options grow and whose ability to refuse shrinks.
- If proposing experimentation, establish how harm will be detected, corrected, and compensated.
- If damage could be irreversible, do not assume the correction model of software updates transfers intact.
- If a technology is harmful in one role, examine alternatives without presuming that redirection always suffices.
- If optimizing a system, preserve its capacity for future learning and participation where possible.

## Key Takeaways
1. The technium is a system of tools and institutions, not a synonym for gadgets.
2. Long-run tendencies and particular social choices must remain distinct.
3. Selective adoption is compatible with technological possibility.
4. Exotropy and the proposed trajectories are Kelly's interpretive framework, not validated universal welfare metrics.
