## Mental Model (read first)
Ask what could become ordinary in people's lives, rather than what looks most futuristic. The authors read early initiatives as bets on new ways to meet needs such as identity, connection, learning, and status; those needs may persist while the successful arrangement changes. Their cases and concluding questions help translate possibility into a lived situation. Multiple companies making similar bets show shared expectations, not proof that the bets will succeed.

## Frameworks & Structure

### Introduction — Near futurism and the distance from novelty to normality
Source: introduction, lines 115–149.

**Near futurism / now-ists:** inspect what is already being attempted and ask whether its consequences are desirable. The authors distinguish technical marvels from innovations people repeatedly use. Their introduction notes that celebrated prototypes can be abandoned, defunded, ignored, or displaced by different ways of meeting the underlying need.

**Slow-changing needs, changing expressions:** the source treats basic wants as more stable than the innovations serving them. Its supersonic-flight discussion considers competing ways of meeting travelers' needs; its pandemic examples show that context-dependent behavior can recede. The library treats stability of need as a working lens, not an exhaustive theory: technologies and institutions may also change expectations and what people value.

**Case architecture:** each chapter moves from a possibility to examples, a featured **Instigator**, and **Imagining the Future Normal** questions. Reuse this distinction between an attested initiative and a possible future. “What if?” questions open design space; they do not constitute a probability estimate or adoption timetable.

**Library application:** identify the person, recurring situation, proposed arrangement, enabling complements, alternative ways to meet the need, and the evidence that would make continued use worthwhile. Keep intended benefit, observed behavior, and anticipated consequences in separate sentences. Ask who can modify, contest, or decline the arrangement.

### Part I — Identity, trust, and learning (chapters 1, 3, 4)
Source: **Multiversal Identity**, lines 159–211; **Certified Media**, 273–333; **Stealth Learning**, 335–395.

- **Multiversal Identity / Ready Player Me:** a cross-platform avatar can make self-expression portable across virtual settings. The network complement is acceptance by developers and platforms, not merely an attractive avatar generator. Ask what the person wants to carry across contexts and what they prefer to keep separate; interoperability and selective disclosure can pull in different directions.
- **Certified Media / Truepic:** recording and verifying an image's origin aims to make authenticity checks part of ordinary media use. The book describes capture metadata and integration into apps and hardware. Editorial boundary: origin evidence does not by itself establish that a caption, interpretation, or depicted claim is true. Adoption requires usable verification and trusted participants throughout the relevant workflow.
- **Stealth Learning / Roblox:** learning can take place inside media and activities people already enjoy, including creating and collaborating rather than only consuming. The opportunity is an activity with useful learning built in. Participation, view counts, and time spent are not proof of retained skill, transfer, or safety; inspect incentives and demonstrate performance beyond the activity itself.

These cases turn an abstract capability into a change in routine: presenting oneself, checking a claim, or learning while making something. Each also makes a new dependency visible—platform compatibility, verification infrastructure, or a learning environment's incentives.

### Part I — Connection through different arrangements (chapters 5–6)
Source: **Ending Loneliness**, lines 397–458; **Virtual Companionship**, 460–524.

**Ending Loneliness / SällBo:** the book's intergenerational housing example combines a mix of residents, shared facilities, and an explicit expectation of neighborly interaction. It shows that service design and institutional organization can create an opportunity without a new technical invention. Housing access, facilitation, personal space, and willingness to participate are causal complements, not decorative details.

**Virtual Companionship / Woebot Health and other examples:** conversational systems may feel available and less judgmental, especially where human support is hard to access. The source also records unease about replacing human contact and adding another digital intervention. These are dated service cases, not clinical recommendations or current evidence of treatment effectiveness.

**Preserve the alternative:** a need for connection could support shared housing, social routines, a digital companion, or a combination. Do not infer that a digital route is universally inauthentic, or that attachment to it establishes improved well-being. Ask which relationships and practical choices the arrangement expands or displaces.

### Part II — Recompose work and creativity (chapters 11–13)
Source: **Augmented Creativity**, lines 762–844; **Remote Work for All**, 846–900; **Work Deconstructed**, 902–954.

- **Augmented Creativity / GitHub's AI Copilot:** the 2023 case highlights assistance with production and anticipates a greater role for assessing generated work. Distinguish output speed from the user's command of the task. Licensing, attribution, review effort, and the formation of expertise can determine whether assistance produces the intended benefit. The book's old product price and uptake numbers are not current benchmarks.
- **Remote Work for All / Einride:** remote human operation offers an alternative to both physical presence and a fully human-free vision. The interesting change is the allocation of work between machine, remote operator, and local environment. Reliable connectivity, handover, situational awareness, and accountability must work as a service; a demonstration does not settle these conditions.
- **Work Deconstructed / job sharing and Zurich Insurance:** redefine a role so two people can share its responsibilities rather than making each choose between full-time presence and a stalled career. The authors describe paired professionals with overlap time, and job adverts offering part-time, full-time, or job-share arrangements. Coordination, promotion norms, client continuity, and shared authority determine whether flexibility becomes real.

**Decision consequence:** when a team says “adopt or reject AI,” consider a role or service redesign as another option. The technology can be useful while the original staffing, incentive, or ownership arrangement is unsuitable. Do not attribute measured recruitment changes to long-term performance or well-being without evidence.

### Part II — Cultural promises require operating arrangements (chapters 14, 16, 20)
Source: **Reflective Cultures**, lines 956–1006; **Impact Hubs**, 1066–1114; **Secondhand Status**, 1262–1314.

- **Reflective Cultures / Black in Fashion Council:** representation in advertising can coexist with weak internal development and decision power. The source contrasts visible branding with a multiyear commitment to share workforce data. For an opportunity assessment, ask what recurring practice, resource allocation, or authority changes rather than accepting an inclusive campaign as the outcome.
- **Impact Hubs / 3Space's BuyGiveWork:** paid workspace helps support space for qualifying community groups, with additional commitments about wages and donated time. The mechanism is a combination of space, subsidy, rules, and relationships. Test whether the commercial base sustains access and whether the intended local groups actually benefit.
- **Secondhand Status / Reflaunt:** resale becomes easier when collection, valuation, photography, listings, and distribution are organized as a service. Trust and convenience can matter as much as the resale interface. A network of marketplaces adds reach while also adding fees, dependencies, authentication burdens, and choices about customer ownership. More resale does not automatically mean less total consumption.

### Part III — Ownership, infrastructure, and environmental outcomes (chapters 21, 28, 30)
Source: **New Collectivism**, lines 1324–1374; **Millions of Microgrids**, 1708–1766; **Beyond Net Zero**, 1830–1890.

**New Collectivism / Exit to Community (E2C):** businesses creating value with a community might eventually become owned by that community. The book emphasizes ownership as a possible destination: early startup uncertainty may make a mature community and distributed governance impractical at inception. Determine who contributed, who receives meaningful rights, and how the transition is financed and governed. Token ownership or a voting interface alone does not establish equitable control. The chapter explicitly questions insider enrichment and utopian Web3 claims; its regulatory descriptions are historical, not current legal guidance.

**Millions of Microgrids / Powerwall and virtual power plants:** local generation and storage can be coordinated into a wider electricity service. The 2021–2022 example depends on opt-in participation and a utility program, showing that distributed assets can need shared rules and central coordination. Evaluate local costs, reliability, interconnection, compensation, and exit terms with current evidence. A participant's reported high-earning week is not a general return forecast.

**Beyond Net Zero / IKEA:** design changes such as easier disassembly can support reuse, while supplier energy choices address a different part of the footprint. Keep a stated climate-positive ambition separate from actual progress and system-wide totals. A per-product improvement, a redesigned joint, and a company target are three different kinds of evidence.

## Worked Example
**SällBo as a designed opportunity for connection** — source case, lines 413–436. The book describes 51 apartments occupied by older adults, younger adults, and some young people who arrived as refugees. Private apartments sit alongside shared kitchens and activity spaces. Residents agree to socialize with neighbors for at least two hours weekly; a municipal nonprofit housing organization supports the arrangement. The book reports positive resident accounts and conversion of the experiment to a permanent project at the start of 2023.

The mechanism is repeated, accessible contact across groups, supported by space and expectations. The case does not isolate which component caused the reported experience or show universal suitability. A hypothetical local adaptation should test demand and accessibility, privacy, voluntary participation, facilitation costs, and whether meaningful relationships develop beyond required attendance. Offer less intensive alternatives for people who prefer them. These tests are the library's synthesis, not a protocol reported by the authors.

## Decision Rules & Judgment

- If an innovation looks impressive, translate it into a recurring human situation before judging its opportunity.
- If a behavior arose under a temporary constraint, test whether its motivating need still requires that form after the constraint changes.
- If a technical solution struggles, compare service, role, space, or ownership redesigns that might meet the same need.
- If a case reports adoption or testimonials, do not silently turn them into causal evidence of benefit.
- If a community creates value, ask whether participation includes control and a sustainable return, and when shared ownership becomes feasible.
- If a promise concerns inclusion, learning, health, or climate, examine the corresponding practice and outcome, not a proxy such as branding, use, or output.
- If making a present recommendation, verify availability and relevant conditions; use the book to formulate hypotheses, not to certify today's providers.

## Key Takeaways
1. The future becomes ordinary through workable practices and complements.
2. Persistent needs can support competing technical and social arrangements.
3. Early cases are starting evidence for design and investigation, not guaranteed adoption or benefit.
