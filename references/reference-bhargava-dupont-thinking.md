## Mental Model (read first)
Useful originality starts by making room to notice, then collecting insight, selecting what matters, and changing the frame. **SIFT** means **Space, Insight, Focus, Twist**, expressed by the part headings **Create Space**, **Uncover Insights**, **Focus Your Ideas**, and **Define the Twist**. It is not the unrelated information-verification method with the same acronym. The goal is a consequential alternative, not novelty or disagreement for its own sake.

## Frameworks & Structure

### Part I — Create Space (chapters 1–6)
Source: lines 197–495. These practices vary inputs and loosen habitual judgment before selecting an answer.

- **Start With Breathing:** bring deliberate attention to an automatic activity. The book uses breathing as an entry to mental space; do not turn its physiological stories into a prescribed breathing regimen or a guaranteed creativity effect.
- **Ditch Your Prebuttals:** postpone a counterargument formed before hearing the actual claim. Ask whether an old impression is doing the work of present evidence. Delayed judgment leaves room to understand; it does not require accepting the eventual claim.
- **Make Oasis Moments:** arrange a brief interruption of overload, either by stepping aside or attending to one detail amid activity. The function is enough space to notice and connect, not a universal retreat or silence requirement.
- **Embrace Danger:** encounter unfamiliar tasks and reduce unnecessary reliance on guides. For this library, apply as a bounded departure from routine where error is tolerable; a playground anecdote does not justify withdrawing safeguards from consequential work.
- **Change Your Rituals:** observe the first hour of a routine, then alter its sequence or add an unfamiliar input. The chapter also offers Mel Robbins's **Five-Second Rule** as a starting cue. Neither ritual stability nor constant change is inherently the right choice; use the variation to expose what habit conceals.
- **Allow Time Spaciousness:** distinguish real external deadlines from arbitrary personal timetables. Reduce unnecessary hurry so observation has time to develop. This does not cancel delivery obligations, resource limits, or the need to decide.

### Part II — Uncover Insights (chapters 7–12)
Source: lines 497–779. An insight attempts to explain why the observed situation takes its form; its surprise does not settle its truth.

- **Ask Story Questions:** invite a concrete episode and build the next question from the answer. Investigate apparent afterthoughts and practice **Question Trees**, successive related questions. Situational detail can expose needs and frictions that a prewritten interview script misses.
- **Hone Your Nunchi:** attend to relational and contextual cues beyond explicit speech; the chapter connects Korean nunchi with reading the room. Treat an inferred intention as something to check, not mind-reading. Cultural and neurodiverse differences make one rigid body-language rule unreliable.
- **Get Your Hands Dirty:** experience or shadow the work that a proposal would change. The book's executive-driver and HVAC ride-along cases expose friction hidden by managerial distance. A short visit supplies hypotheses and questions, not the expertise or representativeness of sustained practice.
- **Spot Natural Wisdom:** use **biomimicry**, adapting an observed natural mechanism, while alternating system-level inspection with fine detail. Ask what functional relationship could transfer. Ecological or biological effectiveness does not prove that a human organization should use the same arrangement.
- **Find the Right Room:** seek conversations with different experiences and ways of seeing, including gatherings outside one's industry. A fashionable network is not automatically informative; choose participants who can reveal a missing part of the problem.
- **Step Into Others’ Shoes:** read the media of unfamiliar communities, follow “un-interests,” and develop **weak ties** beyond the existing social circle. Perspective-taking expands the hypothesis space; confirm imagined concerns with affected people rather than speaking for them.

### Part III — Focus Your Ideas (chapters 13–18)
Source: lines 781–1071. Curating means deciding what deserves attention and what to set aside.

- **Identify the Real Problem:** follow frustrations past the surface complaint. Use the **Five Whys** to ask successive causal questions, checking each answer rather than manufacturing a five-step chain. A capability problem may actually be a trust, coordination, or adoption problem.
- **Discover the Water:** find the condition or shared theme connecting apparently unrelated observations. The chapter reconstructs **Human Mode** from accessible celebrities, employee stories, relatable brands, imperfections, and empathy. A common theme helps focus; it remains possible to group the same examples differently.
- **Be a Satisficer:** set a sufficient standard and commit once it is met instead of continuing an unbounded search for the best option. Keep the standard tied to the task. This is useful for choosing an investigable idea; it does not make an inadequately safe design acceptable.
- **See the Other Side:** describe another plausible perspective on the same situation. **Embrace Dual Truths** and **Tell Situational Backstories** support this move. The duck–rabbit image illustrates alternate construals, not equal truth of all incompatible empirical claims. The source's platypus-as-mammal-and-reptile illustration is erroneous and is excluded.
- **Add Constraints:** restrict scope, time, resources, or form to make selection productive. **Practice the Art of Subtraction** and **Invite Brevity** operationalize the method. A useful constraint sharpens the problem; one that removes an essential beneficiary need should be revised.
- **Use Augmented Creativity:** use a human or technical collaborator to enhance and criticize the idea. The authors describe asking generative AI for hostile reviews and reactions from several audience perspectives. Such simulation is a way to generate objections, not evidence that real audiences hold them or that the system has checked factual accuracy.

### Part IV — Define the Twist (chapters 19–24)
Source: lines 1073–1375. Keep the distinctions among the six moves; they produce different alternatives.

- **Seek Flip Solutions:** identify a rule or sequence, then reverse it. Flipped classrooms move exposition outside class so shared time can support practice and questions; skills demonstrations can replace résumé assertions. Specify what the reversal frees and what support it newly requires.
- **Find Option C:** challenge a binary choice by designing an alternative that satisfies neglected requirements. The chapter draws on **TRIZ**, the Theory of Inventive Problem-Solving, and its attention to contradiction; it does not supply a complete TRIZ procedure. A hybrid is one possibility, not an automatic compromise between two inadequate options.
- **Practice Enigmatology:** use the puzzle-maker's craft to rearrange something familiar so it prompts a new way of seeing. The changed structure or affordance must improve the experience; difficulty, surprise, and a catchy clue do not establish usefulness by themselves.
- **Think Un-Whatever:** define an offering against a category assumption. Unlike flip thinking's reversed procedure, this is positioning by contrast. The author's provocative marketing examples show distinctiveness, not a warrant to manufacture enemies or make false competitor claims.
- **Mind the Intersections:** combine methods or needs from different fields; ask how a different profession would approach the problem. Bank cafés illustrate work, connection, and advice meeting in one setting. A proposal still needs a coherent service mechanism, a user, and evidence that the combination is wanted.
- **Create Your Argot:** use a group's distinctive language, or a short new expression, to make an idea communicable and shared. Naming may change attention and belonging. It must remain intelligible to the people who need to use or challenge the idea.

### Using SIFT without making it a compulsory ritual
Source: introduction, lines 171–185; library application.

Enter where the question is stuck. A narrow binary may need Option C, whereas an evidence-poor interpretation needs observation and story questions before more brainstorming. Develop alternatives that differ in mechanism, beneficiary, or practical arrangement; relabeling one solution three times does not expand interpretation. Focus by asking which difference is consequential and cheap enough to investigate. Preserve an adequate, familiar option when novelty offers no benefit.

The source's illustrations are uneven historical evidence. In particular, an endnote acknowledges dispute over the Netflix late-fee origin story. Neither that story nor an unsupported brain claim is needed for the problem-framing method. Treat simulated perspectives, anecdotes, and analogies as inputs to investigation.

## Worked Example
**Flipped classroom** — chapter 19, lines 1111–1139. The source describes teachers recording exposition for students to watch before class and using shared time for questions, labs, discussion, and individual help. They offered alternative delivery media for students lacking internet or a computer. The twist reverses when exposition and supported practice occur, rather than simply digitizing a lecture.

For a hypothetical professional training program, the library would first identify whether explanation or supported practice is binding. A bounded test could compare a few sessions with equal content and access support, observing preparation burden and performance on a new task. If people cannot prepare or lose needed live explanation, change the arrangement. This proposed test is editorial synthesis; the book's case alone does not prove a general learning advantage.

## Decision Rules & Judgment

- If criticism arrives before the claim is understood, suspend the prebuttal and recover the actual view.
- If everyone reports the same abstraction, ask for a specific episode or observe the work directly.
- If two options share a bad assumption, define a third by changing that assumption rather than averaging them.
- If an idea is clever but irrelevant, return to the beneficiary and actual problem before adding another twist.
- If alternatives multiply without changing the decision, set a sufficient standard and investigate the best candidate.
- If AI supplies a persuasive critique, treat it as a question to verify with source evidence or real users.
- If a natural analogy or story is doing all the evidential work, identify the mechanism and a test before recommending adoption.

## Key Takeaways
1. Space and observation come before confident reinterpretation.
2. Focus preserves standards while limiting search; a twist should change a real choice.
3. Better perspectives generate hypotheses that still need evidence.
